# Decisions

A running log. Each entry is one decision and the reason, in words I can say out loud.

## Scope
- **One buyer, several RFx events, one flow each.** The header lists the buyer's RFx events with their stage (draft, sent, replies in). The demo event carries the five-vendor dataset; a new one can be drafted, sent and filled with replies live.
- **Email is stubbed as an inbox screen.** The brief allows faking the plumbing. Extraction and analysis are real model calls.
- **Left out on purpose:** logins and roles, a vendor portal, negotiation, purchase orders, ERP integration, multi-agent orchestration, vector search.

## Dataset
- **Category: corrugated packaging.** The unit problem is native to it: vendors quote per kg, per piece or per 100, and converting per kg to per piece needs the box weight.
- **The RFx states a box weight for every line.** That makes every per-kg conversion a formula the buyer can check, not a guess.
- **About Rs. 4 crore of annual spend**, to match the stakes in the brief.
- **Each vendor carries one planted problem** (see README). Two vendors miss the quality gate for different reasons: one sent an expired certificate, one sent none.
- **The dataset is generated by a script from one price matrix**, which also writes the true answers. The app never reads the true answers; they exist so I can measure extraction accuracy instead of claiming it.

## Reading the replies
- **The model records what the vendor said; code does the maths.** The model never converts units or currency. Conversion is plain code, so every number on screen has a formula a buyer can check by hand.
- **One model call per vendor, not per file.** The email, the quote and the certificate are read together, because the contradictions live between them (the email says "ISO certified", the certificate says it expired in June).
- **Four reading modes, chosen per file and shown with the reason.** Excel, Word, digital PDF and email are read directly. For images, OCR runs first and its confidence decides the mode.
- **OCR confidence alone is not enough.** On the angled photo OCR scored about 90% on words but scrambled the rows, so it could not say which rate belonged to which box. Tables in images go to the vision model for layout, and OCR's numbers are kept as a second opinion on each digit.
- **The quality gate is decided by code, from dates the model read.** The model reads the expiry date on the certificate; code compares it with today. Three outcomes: cleared, failed, unverified. "Unverified" is separate from "failed" because the buyer's next step differs: chase the document, versus exclude the vendor.
- **Four checks that do not depend on the model's opinion of itself:** the model's own stated doubt; whether the extracted number exists in the source text; whether OCR agrees with vision; and whether the price is more than 35% away from last year's.
- **Photo errors come in runs.** An older model (gpt-4.1) slipped one row near the bottom of the photo and shifted six prices, three of them plausible enough to pass every check. Two fixes: I chose the model by measured accuracy (gpt-5.5 reads all 150 cells correctly), and I added a rule that two implausible prices from one image put every row of that image up for review. With the rule, the older model's six wrong prices are all flagged.
- **The number I watch is "wrong and not flagged".** A wrong price that is flagged costs the buyer a minute. A wrong price that looks confident costs the award. `scripts/run_extraction.py` prints this for every vendor.
- **Buyer corrections survive re-runs.** Changing the FX rate recalculates every cell except the ones a buyer has corrected by hand.

## The comparison screen
- **One grid holds everything a buyer weighs:** totals, commercial terms, questionnaire answers, documents and the 30 lines, with vendors as columns. The brief asks for answers and documents "alongside the numbers", so they are rows in the same table, not separate tabs.
- **Every cell opens its evidence.** A click shows the vendor's own words, the source document with the passage highlighted (or the photo), the conversion formula, how the line was matched, and any reason for doubt.
- **Five cell states, visible without clicking:** quoted, derived (the original unit is printed under the price), assumed (amber), not quoted, and needs review (orange with a warning mark).
- **Two totals, on purpose.** "Everything the vendor priced" flatters a vendor who skipped expensive lines, so the like-for-like basket totals only the lines every vendor priced.
- **Conditional rebates are shown but not applied.** Kraftline's 4% depends on annual offtake, which depends on the award. Applying it silently would make a price look firm when it is not. The analyst can apply it as a scenario.
- **Freight and duty gaps are shown, not estimated.** Three vendors exclude some delivery cost. I do not invent a freight figure; the screen says the prices are not yet like for like.
- **The FX rate is on screen and editable.** Changing it recalculates from the stored extraction without calling the model again.
- **Buyer decisions are logged.** Confirming or correcting a cell records who, what and when, clears the flag, and protects the cell from later recalculation.
- **A shared password protects the hosted link,** because every question spends my API key.

## The analyst
- **An agent, because the questions are open-ended.** Extraction is a fixed pipeline; the analyst chooses its own steps, up to ten per question.
- **The model never does arithmetic.** It has four tools: look up data with SQL, show a table, show a chart, and run an award scenario. Code computes every figure. Tables and charts are built from query results, so the model cannot mistype a number into them.
- **Award scenarios are one deterministic function,** not model reasoning. Its inputs are the levers a buyer actually pulls: which vendors are eligible by quality gate, a cap on vendor count, whether to ignore prices that are assumed or flagged, whether a conditional rebate counts, and a freight or duty estimate per vendor. With a cap it tries every vendor combination, which is cheap at five vendors.
- **Every answer shows its working.** "How this was worked out" lists each query and scenario setting. A buyer or an auditor can rerun them.
- **Caveats travel with the number.** The scenario returns its own caveats (freight excluded, gate not cleared, rebate condition, assumed cells), and the instructions require them next to the figure, not at the bottom.
- **Nothing is assumed on the buyer's behalf.** Rebates and freight estimates are applied only when the buyer asks, and the answer says so.
- **The database connection is read-only,** so no question can change the data.
- **It says when it does not know.** Asked for a vendor's credit rating or an unquoted freight charge, it says the data does not hold it and what to ask the vendor.
- **Exports carry their method.** Each Excel file has a second sheet with the query or scenario settings, the FX rate, the time and the caveats, so the file can be defended after it leaves the tool.

## Drafting and sending the RFx
- **The co-pilot fills a structured draft, not a document.** The buyer talks on the left and the RFx builds on the right: scope, terms, line items, questionnaire and a list of what is still open. Structure is what makes the replies comparable later.
- **It returns only what changed each turn, and code applies it.** The draft on screen is the stored one, not the model's memory of it.
- **It never invents quantities, sizes or weights.** Missing values show as "missing" and go on the open list. It may propose terms and questions, and says they are proposals.
- **It asks for box weight on every line.** This is the lesson from the reply side fed back into the request: with a weight in the RFx, a per-kg quote converts exactly.
- **The outgoing email is built by code from the draft,** so it says exactly what the draft says. The buyer reviews it before sending.
- **Sending is stubbed and says so.** No email leaves the system. Sending turns the draft into a live RFx; because there is no mail server, replies are added as files or pasted text and then read for real.
- **Anyone can add their own vendor reply.** Any file or pasted text goes through the same pipeline and lands in the comparison. This is the proof that extraction is not staged. "Read again" re-runs any seeded reply live for the same reason.
- **The co-pilot works for any category.** A line item is a description, a free-text specification, a unit and a quantity, with unit weight asked for only where vendors quote by weight. The demo dataset stays corrugated because that is where the unit problem is hardest.
- **The buyer can hand over a document instead of typing.** A requirement note in Word, PDF, Excel or a photo is opened by the same readers used for vendor replies, and the co-pilot drafts from it and says what the document did not contain.

## Changes after re-reading the brief
- **"One flow, end to end" was broken at the send step.** A drafted RFx went nowhere, and the replies on screen belonged to a different RFx. Now a drafted RFx becomes a live event of its own, with its own replies, comparison and analyst.
- **Each RFx has its own database file.** Events cannot leak into each other, and the analyst can only see the RFx on screen.
- **A new RFx has no last-year prices and may lack box weights.** The checks that need them switch off for that RFx, and a per-kg quote with no box weight is marked "unclear" instead of guessed.
- **When the system is not sure, it drafts the question to the vendor.** One click turns a vendor's flagged cells and open points into a clarification email of at most eight questions. It is not sent; the buyer reviews it.
- **The buyer can speak.** The brief says the buyer "talks" an RFx into existence, so the co-pilot has a microphone button where the browser supports it.
- **The whole comparison exports to Excel,** with each price's status and a sheet of caveats, because buyers will still need to send a file to someone.
- **The demo can be reset.** Interviewers will correct cells, change the FX rate and upload files; one click restores the demo RFx.

## Navigation, after using it as a buyer would
- **The buyer lands on a dashboard of their RFx events,** each a card with its stage, counts and next step, plus a card to create a new one. Opening a card enters that RFx; "All RFx" goes back. The earlier design dropped the buyer straight into one RFx with no way out.
- **Analyst conversations are saved per RFx,** with a list on the left and a "New conversation" button, as in any chat assistant. A buyer asks about freight today and award splits tomorrow, and needs both.
- **The login page is a sample buyer sign-in.** Only the shared password is checked; real accounts and roles are out of scope.
- **A partly answered mandatory question makes a vendor "unverified", not "failed".** Only a clear "no" or contradicting evidence excludes a vendor.

## Vendor risk
- **The brief does not ask for vendor risk; I added it because price alone does not make an award defensible.** The cheapest vendor in the demo would depend on this contract for 64% of its turnover.
- **Risk is built by rule from what each vendor sent, not from outside data and not by the model.** There is no credit-bureau feed in a prototype, and inventing one would break the rule that every number is traceable.
- **Five dimensions:** quality and compliance, financial standing, supply and delivery, commercial terms, and quote firmness. Each is low, medium, high or "no information", with its reasons written out.
- **Financial standing comes from two questions added to the RFx** (turnover and year established; credit rating). The model reads the answers; code converts and judges. The screen labels them "vendor-declared, not verified".
- **The financial measure that matters to a buyer is dependency:** what share of the vendor's turnover this contract would be. Above 30% is high.
- **"No information" is its own level, in grey.** A vendor who told us nothing is not low risk, and counts as at least medium overall.
- **The overall level is the worst of the five, not an average.** One red should not be averaged away by four greens.
- **It sits in the comparison grid as its own section, with an overall badge under each vendor's name,** because risk is weighed alongside price, not on a separate page.
- **Colours are green, amber, red and grey, always with the word beside them,** so the meaning never rests on colour alone.
- **The analyst knows each vendor's risk** and states it when an answer would award that vendor lines.
